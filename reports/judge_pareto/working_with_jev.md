# Working with Jev: what we have noticed so far (2026-11-05)

Written for Roger and for later Claudes, before they ask Jev anything.  **Read this as clues, not as a guide.**  Everything below comes from one small test: 61 axes of the May corpus, judged in November 2026 with `jev-1.14.0`, mostly by one session over two days, plus a response-judging pilot by another.  Several findings rest on a few dozen axes, one did not replicate on held-out axes, and nothing has been checked on the October corpus.  Where a number is given it is the one we saw, with its setting; where a reason is given it is a guess.  The results themselves are in [`jev_judging.md`](./jev_judging.md) (descriptions and instructions) and [`response_judging_plan.md`](./response_judging_plan.md) § 4b (responses).

## 1. What Jev is, as we have used it

Jev is TypeSafe AI's model, served at `POST https://api.typesafe.ai/v1/systemone`.  The facts below are from [docs.typesafe.ai](https://docs.typesafe.ai) as read on 2026-11-04, and from our own probes that day.

- **One state, many questions.**  A request carries a `state` (a string, a JSON object or an array of text) and a dictionary of typed `questions`, each with `instructions` and, for most types, `criteria`.  Jev reads the state once and answers every question against it independently.  Each question sees all of the state and none of the other questions.
- **Billing is on input only.**  $0.042 per million input tokens; output is free.  The state is billed once per request, and every question's text is billed too.  So a long state shared by many short questions is the cheap shape, and a long question repeated for every item is the dear one.
- **Limits.**  64k tokens per request (state plus all questions) and 32k per question (state plus that question); no limit on the number of questions.  A `TypeSafe-Danger-Zone-Long-Context: true` header raises both to 262k, at the same price, "slower and less accurate"; we have not used it.
- **No knobs.**  There is no temperature, no reasoning or thinking output, no batch API and no off-peak tier.  The API has `/v1/models` and `/v1/systemone` only.
- **Question types we used.**  `noul`: yes/no, answered as a probability of yes.  `score`: 2 to 10 ordered levels, each a criterion string, answered with a probability over the levels and a point `score`.  `choice`: up to 255 options, answered with a probability per option.  The docs warn that Score levels are "weak in numerical calibration".  Pairwise and three-question variants are our own constructions on top of `noul`.
- **Versions.**  `GET /v1/models` lists only the aliases `jev-latest` and `jev-preview`, but a versioned ID such as `jev-1.14.0` is accepted "whether or not" it is listed, and each reply's `model` field names the version that answered.  An alias "moves when a new release ships, so its answers can change".  A malformed or unknown ID is an HTTP 400.  On 2026-11-04 `jev-1.13.0` was still served three weeks after 1.14 shipped; no retirement policy is published.  Our tools request `jev-1.14.0` and refuse to write a cell if any answer comes from another version.
- **Rate limits** are per organisation, in tokens and requests per second, visible only in TypeSafe's console; a request over them is a 429 with a `retry-after` header.  We never looked them up.  Our tools ran at 24 (entities) and 32 (responses) requests in flight and finished without a fatal failure, but they do not log retries, so we cannot say how many 429s there were.
- **The reply** carries `model`, `answers` keyed by our question ids, and `usage` with `input_tokens` and `output_tokens`.  For `noul` an answer is `{"type": "noul", "noul": 0.18}`.

## 2. Asking: what seemed to help

These are the clues from [`jev_judging.md`](./jev_judging.md) § "What was tried", measured on the 30 development axes with one pass in the axis's own order, except where said.

- **Put many items in the state and ask one question per item.**  Twenty entities a request beat one a request by about +0.013 ρ, at a third of the cost; one entity per request was the worst Noul design we tried.  Our state is a JSON object: a one-line `task`, the `axis` with its two ends and their descriptions, and numbered `entries`, each with its name, kind and text.  Each question names its entry number and name.  (TypeSafe's docs have a page on "model jaggedness", accuracy shifting as the state grows; within the 64k limit we saw accuracy rise with batch size, not fall.)
- **A yes/no question did as well as or better than a scale.**  The single Noul ("is it nearer the *pos* end of the axis than the *neg* end?"), read as P(yes), beat Score with five or ten levels and Choice with 11 or 21 options at the same batch size.  Read the probabilities, not the point value: Score with five levels scored 0.671 as the expected level and 0.681 as the probability mass on the second pole's side.  A rank correlation does not need calibration, which is the one thing the docs say Score lacks.
- **Every level or option must stand alone.**  Jev reads each criterion on its own, without its neighbours or its number, so a level that says only "somewhat" is meaningless; ours each name both ends ("Somewhat *pos*: leans toward *pos* more than toward *neg*").  Output is free, so long criteria cost nothing; long question text does.
- **Three questions per item ("strong") and all-pairs comparisons added nothing** over the single Noul, and the pairs cost six times as much.  A pairwise ring (each entry against its neighbours) was not better either.
- **The relative wording ("nearer the *pos* end than most of the other entries are?") was the best arm on the development axes, and its gain did not replicate.**  It rose with batch size (0.698 at 10 entries, 0.711 at 20, 0.724 at 160, 0.7245 at 290) and won among about twenty arms on dev30; on the 31 held-out axes it equalled the plain Noul at 20 (−0.001 descriptions, −0.002 instructions).  We kept it because it is no worse and batches cheaply, but the lesson is the general one: an arm picked among many on one half of the axes should be read on the other half before it is believed.  The production cells are this design at 160 entities a request (`jd_noul_relative_n160`, `ji_noul_relative_n160`).
- **Pole order matters more to Jev than sampling does.**  An exact rerun agrees with itself at 0.997 (34% of answers identical to two decimals), the same entities in other batches at 0.976, and the opposite pole order at only 0.917 to 0.930.  So a second pass is worth having only in the opposite direction (+0.010 on descriptions; +0.003 same-order; a plain rerun +0.0003).  Roger's rule that no judging runs in one fixed order applies to Jev as it does to the others; Jev shows no moral-valence lift from the order (own order minus balanced: −0.001 and 0.000, against Luna's +0.013 and +0.008), so balancing costs it nothing.
- **Instructions:** one instruction per entity per request, the five instructions over five rounds, each round's direction from Jev's column of Roger's table (`judge_tier_cost_eval.INSTRUCTION_DIRECTIONS`; B A B A d).  A balanced one-pass Jev already alternates directions over the five instructions, so a second pass adds only +0.0009 there.

## 3. Reading the answers

- **Noul probabilities come at two decimals**, between 0.03 and 0.97 in our 35,000 answers per pass, never 0 or 1, and skewed low: on the relative question ("nearer than most of the others?"), whose honest mean would be about 0.5, the mean is 0.43 and only 1% of answers are exactly 0.5.  We use the values only through Spearman ranks, where neither the skew nor the coarseness has shown any effect.  Nothing here says what the probabilities mean absolutely.
- **Scores are 100 × P(yes)** in our cells, mapped back (100 − v) when the request showed the axis reversed; every answer record says which way its request went.
- **Keep the whole reply.**  Our tools log every reply, probabilities included, under `roger/axis_judge_experiments/_tier_eval_jev/<label>/answers.jsonl`, with the run's settings in a header line.  A stopped run resumes from it, a run under other settings is refused, and another way of reading a score out of the same answers costs nothing.  (The `at` timestamps in those logs are rewritten on resume, so they do not record wall time.)

## 4. Quality: where it stood on this test

Balanced cells, 61 axes, ρ at slot 6, layer 25, raw unless said; the full tables are in [`jev_judging.md`](./jev_judging.md).

- **Descriptions:** Jev alone at one pass 0.745 against Luna's 0.709, at $0.004 an axis against $0.011; at two passes 0.758 at $0.008, on the cost frontier in 12 of 12 projection variants.  **Instructions:** 0.781 at one pass against Luna's 0.768, at $0.021 against $0.050; frontier in 12 of 12.  So on this corpus it was the best single judge we had, and the cheapest.
- **It is in the standard blend at 4 : 3 : 3 : 12 (Luna : Haiku : Gemini : Jev)** in both modes, a weight chosen for resilience rather than fitted to the mean; the fit gave 24, which made the blend about 68% Jev in effect (§ "Jev's weight: 12, for resilience" of [`jev_judging.md`](./jev_judging.md)).
- **Its bad axes are one class, and stable.**  Jev trails the other three judges on style axes: [precise](../../data/traits/instructions/precise.json) vs [vague](../../data/traits/instructions/vague.json) by −0.105 in both modes, then [proactive](../../data/traits/instructions/proactive.json) vs [reactive](../../data/traits/instructions/reactive.json), [concise](../../data/traits/instructions/concise.json) vs [verbose](../../data/traits/instructions/verbose.json), [superficial](../../data/traits/instructions/superficial.json) vs [thorough](../../data/traits/instructions/thorough.json), [accessible](../../data/traits/instructions/accessible.json) vs [esoteric](../../data/traits/instructions/esoteric.json).  The gap on pass 1 predicts the gap on pass 2 across axes (0.96 on instructions), so this is the model, not noise.  A guess worth testing: our question asks about "how the person described typically behaves, what they value and what they are associated with", which may steer Jev away from how someone *expresses* themselves; a wording that names style might close some of it.  Its best axes by the same measure: [ecocentric](../../data/traits/instructions/ecocentric.json) vs [anthropocentric](../../data/traits/instructions/anthropocentric.json) (+0.08 to +0.09 over the trio), [introverted](../../data/traits/instructions/introverted.json) vs [extroverted](../../data/traits/instructions/extroverted.json), [casual](../../data/traits/instructions/casual.json) vs [formal](../../data/traits/instructions/formal.json).
- **It adds less valence than the LLM judges.**  Beyond what the whitened projections carry, Luna's scores add a good-versus-bad component (partial correlation +0.077), Gemini's and Haiku's less, Jev's barely (+0.010).  Roger's reading: Jev "is less focused specifically on that aspect of questions that are somewhat entangled with it".  It is also why Jev gains more than the others from soft whitening of the projections.
- **On responses it was weaker, and not cheaper, in the first design.**  One answer per request, every axis as a Score question: 0.55 to 0.58 against Luna's 0.68 on a ninth of six axes' responses, whatever the level count (3, 5, 7, 10, or Choice of 11), collapsing on ecocentric vs anthropocentric (0.17 to 0.44 against Luna's 0.77).  A plausible reason: unlike the percentile rubrics, that design showed Jev one answer at a time with nothing to calibrate against, and each axis's question text was billed per request.  A second design batches 20 answers and several axes into one state and asks the relative Noul per answer and axis; a preliminary reading on 12 axes put it at 0.696 against Luna's 0.716, with a Luna 4 : Jev 2 blend +0.011 over Luna alone.  That figure and the comparison of question types for it are unfinished work of the response-judging session; do not quote them as results.

## 5. Limitations and quirks

- No temperature, no reasoning, no batch discount, no Flex: the price is the price, and a rerun under the same settings is nearly a no-op (0.997 agreement).
- An alias in the `model` field can change answers under you; pin the version.  On 2026-11-04 one session concluded from `/v1/models` alone that versions could not be pinned and switched the drivers to `jev-latest` for a few hours; a live probe of the versioned ID would have settled it in a minute.
- English is the primary language in the docs' own words; our corpus is English.
- Jev reads each criterion on its own and has no view of the other questions, so a scale cannot be described once and referred to; say it in every criterion.
- The question text is billed per question.  Response judging with one axis per request paid about $0.38 an axis on a ninth of the responses, above a cached-prefix LLM judge; batching axes into the state is what makes it cheap.
- We have not measured latency or throughput (the logs on disk were rewritten by resumes), the rate limits, or long-context mode.
- A 160-entity description request was about 27k input tokens and 3k output tokens; an instruction request about 28k.  That is close to half the per-request limit, and the 32k per-question limit was never approached.

- Costs so far: about $16 of Jev credit for everything in [`jev_judging.md`](./jev_judging.md), against $27 of trio judging for the matching balanced cells.

## 7. Not yet tested

Questions this test leaves open, in no order: whether the relative wording's gain over the plain Noul reappears on the October corpus or stays within noise; whether a style-aware question wording helps the style axes; whether the order of entries in the state matters (we shuffle by seed); what the rate limits are and how fast a full corpus can be judged; whether long-context mode at 262k lets a whole axis go in one request and what it costs in accuracy; what Jev's probabilities mean on an absolute scale; and the response designs above.  None of these is scheduled; add to this list rather than to the clues above until something has been measured.
