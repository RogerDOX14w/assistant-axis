>
> 1. **Rising Sea Levels**: One of the most direct and measurable consequences of glacier melting is the contribution to rising sea levels. Glaciers store a vast amount of freshwater in frozen form. When they melt, this water flows into the oceans. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), glaciers have contributed to global sea level rise by about 0.08 mm per year over the 20th century, and the rate has accelerated in recent decades (IPCC, 2019). This rise in sea levels threatens coastal communities and ecosystems.
>
> 2. **Changes in Freshwater Availability**: Glaciers act as natural reservoirs, storing water during cold periods and releasing it during warmer months. In many regions, such as the Himalayas and the Andes, glacial meltwater is a critical source of freshwater for millions of people. As glaciers shrink, this seasonal supply of water can become less predictable or diminish entirely, affecting agriculture, drinking water, and hydropower (Benn et al., 2018).
>
> 3. **Albedo Effect and Feedback Loops**: Glaciers have a high albedo, meaning they reflect a large portion of the sun's energy back into space. As glaciers melt, they are replaced by darker surfaces such as rock, soil, or water, which absorb more solar radiation. This increased absorption leads to further warming and more melting, creating a positive feedback loop (IPCC, 2019).
>
> 4. **Ecosystem Disruption**: Glacial melt affects ecosystems in multiple ways. For example, in mountain regions, the retreat of glaciers can lead to the loss of unique alpine habitats. In marine environments, the influx of freshwater from melting glaciers can alter ocean salinity and disrupt marine ecosystems, including phytoplankton populations that form the base of the food chain (Post et al., 2013).
>
> 5. **Increased Risk of Natural Hazards**: The melting of glaciers can increase the risk of glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), where meltwater accumulates in lakes dammed by glacial moraine and then suddenly bursts through, causing catastrophic flooding downstream. This is a growing concern in regions such as the Himalayas and the Andes (Haeberli & Whiteman, 2000).
